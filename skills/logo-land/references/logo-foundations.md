# What the logo must do

Read this when developing a brand direction, diagnosing weak results or deciding
whether to refine a candidate. These are design judgments informed by primary
sources, not aesthetic laws or a scoring formula.

## Identify before explaining

A logo gives an organization a recognizable sign. It need not illustrate its
business or encode every value in a hidden metaphor. Its associations also develop
through use alongside the organization's work. Paul Rand makes this distinction in
[Logos, Flags, and Escutcheons](https://www.paulrand.design/writing/articles/1991-logos-flags-and-escutcheons.html).

An abstract mark can be appropriate without immediately saying “software studio.”
Conversely, an ordinary shape does not become distinctive because its description
claims courage, connection or creativity. Record what the image actually shows
before attaching a meaning to it.

The logo is one part of an identity. Existing typography, naming, colors and layout
can supply context without being redesigned. Pentagram's
[Mastercard project](https://www.pentagram.com/work/mastercard) shows the relationship
between the mark and a larger visual system. When the user asks for a symbol and
the typeface is settled, make a symbol. Use the actual existing text asset in a
separate context preview if available; generating similar-looking lettering does
not preserve a font.

## Ask questions that change the next action

| Question | Look for in the actual image | What does not establish it |
|---|---|---|
| What identifies this mark? | A specific contour, proportion, rhythm or relationship between solid and empty areas | Few shapes, one color, a named metaphor or “premium” in the prompt |
| Does it suit this organization? | Its visible character in the documented brand and use context; conflicting genre cues | A literal industry pictogram or an invented explanation after generation |
| Does simplification preserve its character? | The feature that remains when incidental decoration is removed | Reducing every brief to a circle, swoosh or geometric initial |
| Is the form resolved? | Deliberate curves, joins, gaps, terminals and optical weight | Grid alignment, symmetry or decorative construction diagrams alone |
| Does it work where needed? | The actual mark at the required size, background and neighboring elements | A large centered image, an unviewed HTML preview or a nominal CSS size |
| What can be delivered honestly? | The real file, inspected variants and known editing limits | Calling a raster concept a vector master or a successful test a good design |

Figma's [logo design introduction](https://www.figma.com/resource-library/how-to-design-a-logo/)
treats brand understanding, exploration, testing and revision as parts of the
process. Simplicity helps only when the important relationships survive. It is not
a requirement to make every brand minimal, monochrome, corporate or generic.

An expert's judgment about distinction is not proof of consumer recall, market
uniqueness or trademark clearance. Name the comparison scope and unknowns rather
than claiming those studies happened.

## Refine relationships, not adjectives

Perceived balance can require unequal measurements. IBM's
[8-bar guidance](https://www.ibm.com/design/language/ibm-logos/8-bar/) describes optical
differences between positive and reversed artwork. Google's account of
[its identity evolution](https://design.google/library/evolving-google-identity)
describes adjustments to the compact G. These examples support inspecting a
specific join, weight or gap; their geometry is not a recipe to copy.

Use the real display condition. A 32px image canvas with generous empty margins
may contain a much smaller symbol. Record the viewed canvas size and whether the
mark was cropped or fitted. Do not assert a minimum usable size from a size label.
Color roles also need visual inspection together: correct HEX values do not prove
that neighboring shapes remain distinct or that the accent has the right weight.

## Separate exploration from production

Native image generation can explore form and produce raster revisions. Selecting
a promising image starts refinement; it does not establish a production master.
Adobe's [modern logo process](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/design/discover/modern-logo-design.html)
moves from research and broad sketches toward selected, editable artwork with
feedback. A natural stopping point for an image-only tool may therefore be an
inspected concept plus a precise construction handoff.

If vector work is requested and available, inspect the actual paths, silhouette,
joins and small gaps after conversion. Figma's
[vector simplification guidance](https://help.figma.com/hc/en-us/articles/33792593975575-Simplify-a-vector-path)
explains why removing excess points can help but removing too much can damage the
form. An SVG filename or automatic trace is not proof of clean construction.
